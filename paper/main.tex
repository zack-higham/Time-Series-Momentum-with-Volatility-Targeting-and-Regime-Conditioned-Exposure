\begin{abstract}
\noindent
This paper implements the time-series momentum (TSMOM) strategy of \citet{moskowitz2012} on ten liquid exchange-traded funds spanning equities, government bonds, commodities and currencies, over 222 months from April 2008 to September 2026, and decomposes its performance into three layers: the trend signal, volatility targeting, and a regime-conditioned exposure overlay based on a walk-forward Markov-switching model. Every design parameter was committed to version control before any result was computed. The raw equal-notional strategy earns a Sharpe ratio of 0.26 net of costs. Scaling positions by inverse volatility and targeting 10\% ex-ante portfolio volatility raises the Sharpe ratio to 0.45 (Newey-West $t = 1.95$) and turns monthly skewness from $-0.50$ to $+0.33$, but the improvement over the raw strategy is not statistically significant (bootstrap $p = 0.19$). The volatility-targeted portfolio has the defining property of trend following: it gained 9.2\%, 12.6\% and 30.6\% over the three largest equity drawdowns of the sample (2008 to 2009, 2020 and 2022) while the S\&P 500 fell between 25\% and 52\%, and it averaged $+1.8\%$ in the worst decile of equity months. It is also highly correlated with the AQR futures TSMOM factor ($R^2 = 0.60$), which validates the ETF implementation. The regime overlay does not add value: three walk-forward versions lower the Sharpe ratio by 0.02 to 0.13, and the model's ``trending'' state does not forecast trend returns out of sample, whereas a version using full-sample smoothed probabilities would have reported a spurious gain of 0.12. After deflating for the 25 configurations examined, the primary strategy's Sharpe ratio is not significant at the 5\% level. In a portfolio application specified before it was computed, holding 20\% in the strategy alongside 80\% in a 60/40 portfolio reduced the maximum drawdown from 32.3\% to 25.3\% and the loss in each of the six equity crisis windows, and raised the Sharpe ratio from 0.74 to 0.86, a gain that is not statistically significant (bootstrap $p = 0.08$). The evidence supports trend following as a diversifier with positive crisis convexity, not as a reliably profitable stand-alone strategy over this sample.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Time-series momentum is the tendency of an asset's own past return to predict its future return. \citet{moskowitz2012} (MOP) document it across 58 futures contracts from 1985 to 2009: a portfolio that is long each contract whose trailing twelve-month excess return is positive and short each contract whose return is negative earns a large and persistent premium, with its strongest returns in extreme market moves. \citet{hurst2017} extend the evidence back over a century. Trend following of this kind is the core business of managed-futures funds, and its central selling point is ``crisis alpha'': positive returns during prolonged equity sell-offs, when diversification is most valuable \citep{fung2001}.

Two refinements are standard in practice. The first is volatility targeting: positions are scaled inversely to their ex-ante volatility so that each contributes comparable risk, and the whole portfolio is scaled to a constant volatility target. MOP use the first step; \citet{kim2016} argue that much of the TSMOM premium is in fact a volatility-scaling effect, and \citet{moreira2017} and \citet{harvey2018} study volatility management more broadly. The second refinement is regime conditioning: varying exposure according to an estimated market state, in the spirit of the regime-switching models of \citet{hamilton1989} and \citet{ang2002}. The premise is that trend strategies earn their returns in persistent ``trending'' regimes and lose in choppy, mean-reverting ones.

This paper asks four questions about a TSMOM strategy built only from liquid ETFs over 2008 to 2026, a period widely regarded as difficult for trend followers. Does the strategy earn a positive risk-adjusted return? How much of its performance comes from the signal, from volatility targeting, and from a regime overlay? Does it deliver crisis alpha in the sample's equity drawdowns? And how robust is any of this to implementation choices, costs and multiple testing?

The design is deliberately conservative. Every parameter (universe, sample, signal horizon, volatility estimator, risk allocation, cost assumption and regime model) was fixed in a configuration file and committed to version control before the first backtest was run, so the repository history documents that nothing was tuned to the results. The primary results are reported for these settings; alternatives appear only as labelled robustness checks. Where a decision had to be made after results were seen, it is identified as such.

The main findings are as follows. Predictability at the instrument level is weak: none of the pooled regressions of returns on lagged signals is significant at 5\%. The traded portfolio nonetheless earns a positive return, and volatility targeting is the layer that matters: it raises the Sharpe ratio from 0.26 to 0.45 and turns negative skewness positive, although the gain is not statistically significant. The volatility-targeted strategy gained in each of the three largest equity drawdowns and tracks the AQR futures TSMOM factor closely. Held at a fixed 20\% weight beside a 60/40 portfolio, it lowered the portfolio's maximum drawdown and its loss in every equity crisis window, although the accompanying rise in the Sharpe ratio is not statistically significant. The regime overlay, by contrast, adds nothing out of sample, and a smoothed-probability version of the same overlay shows how easily a lookahead error would have suggested otherwise. Once the number of configurations examined is taken into account, the primary Sharpe ratio is not distinguishable from what selection alone could produce. The contribution is a transparent, reproducible decomposition that separates what the data support from what sampling variation and research design could manufacture.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

\textbf{Universe.} The universe is ten U.S.-listed ETFs in four asset classes: equities (SPY, S\&P 500; EFA, developed markets excluding North America; EEM, emerging markets), U.S.\ Treasuries (IEF, 7 to 10 years; TLT, 20 years and over), commodities (GLD, physical gold; DBC, a diversified commodity futures basket) and currencies (UUP, long the U.S.\ dollar against the DXY basket; FXY, long the yen; FXA, long the Australian dollar). The currency sleeve uses three differentiated exposures: the broad dollar, a safe-haven currency and a commodity-linked currency. A euro fund was rejected because the euro is about 58\% of the dollar index, making it close to a duplicate of UUP (daily correlation $-0.95$). Short-duration Treasuries were excluded because their volatility of about 1.5\% would require extreme leverage under volatility targeting, and single-commodity funds were excluded as redundant with DBC and GLD.

\textbf{Prices.} Daily closing prices, dividend- and split-adjusted total-return prices, volume and every distribution and split were downloaded from Yahoo Finance from 2 January 2002 to 30 September 2026. The adjusted series was verified by rebuilding total returns from closing prices and distributions: the largest annual discrepancy across the ten funds is 6 basis points. The panel has no interior gaps and no non-positive prices, and every daily move larger than 8\% (49 in all) was matched to an identifiable market event, such as the 27 February 2007 Shanghai sell-off, the March 2020 crash and the 30 January 2026 fall in gold.

\textbf{Risk-free rate and excess returns.} ETFs are fully funded, whereas the futures used in the TSMOM literature earn excess returns. Every return in this paper (in signals, volatility estimates and profit and loss) is therefore an excess return over the 13-week Treasury bill rate (Yahoo Finance \texttt{\^{}IRX}). The daily rate is the annualised yield known at the previous close, accrued over calendar days on an actual/360 basis. Compounded to months, it has a correlation of 0.991 with the Kenneth French one-month bill series. Subtracting the bill rate matters: in 2023 and 2024 cash earned about 5\% a year, and without it both the signals and the profit and loss of short positions would be misstated. Monthly excess returns are the month-end to month-end total return minus the compounded bill return over the same days.

\textbf{Sample.} UUP begins trading in March 2007, so the first month-end at which all ten instruments have a twelve-month signal is 31 March 2008. The main sample is the 222 complete months from April 2008 to September 2026. Earlier data is used only for estimation warm-up and the regime model's training burn-in (Section~\ref{sec:regime_method}).

\textbf{Benchmark data.} The AQR Time Series Momentum factor, constructed from futures and forwards across the same four asset classes following the MOP methodology, is used as an external benchmark. It ends in May 2026, so comparisons with it cover 218 months.

Table~\ref{tab:data} summarises the instruments. SPY has the highest Sharpe ratio (0.72). The bond funds earned little in excess of cash over the sample (1.52\% a year for TLT and 1.12\% for IEF), because the 2022 sell-off reversed much of the earlier bond rally, and the yen lost 3.87\% a year against the dollar in excess-return terms. Table~\ref{tab:corr} reports monthly correlations. Within asset classes they are high (SPY and EFA 0.87, IEF and TLT 0.92), and the three currency funds are not independent (UUP and FXA $-0.78$). The effective number of independent bets within each class, measured by the eigenvalue participation ratio of its correlation matrix, is 1.26 for equities, 1.08 for rates, 1.87 for commodities and 1.93 for currencies. This motivates the risk allocation in Section~\ref{sec:vt}.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Timing convention}

All signals and weights are computed from information available at the close of the last trading day of month $t$, and the resulting positions are held through month $t+1$. Trades execute at that same close, as in MOP; a one-day execution lag is reported as a robustness check. Between rebalances, positions drift with prices and the portfolio is marked to market daily, so that drawdowns inside a month (most importantly March 2020) are measured.

\subsection{Signals}

Let $r_{i,d}$ be the daily total return of instrument $i$ and $r^f_d$ the bill return. The $k$-month signal at month-end $t$ is the sign of the cumulative excess return over the preceding $k$ months,
\begin{equation}
s^{(k)}_{i,t} = \operatorname{sign}\!\left( \frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,t-k}} - \prod_{d \in (t-k,\,t]} \bigl(1 + r^f_d\bigr) \right),
\end{equation}
where $P_{i,t}$ is the total-return price at month-end $t$. Four horizons are tested, $k \in \{1, 3, 6, 12\}$, together with an equal-weighted blend of the four signs. The twelve-month signal is the primary specification, fixed in advance from MOP; the other horizons are reported so that the choice can be judged, not so that the best one can be chosen. Following MOP, no month is skipped: in time-series momentum the most recent month is part of the trend.

\subsection{Ex-ante volatility}

Volatility is the exponentially weighted estimator of MOP,
\begin{equation}
\sigma^2_{i,t} = 252 \sum_{j \ge 0} (1-\delta)\,\delta^{j} \bigl(x_{i,t-j} - \bar{x}_{i,t}\bigr)^2, \qquad \frac{\delta}{1-\delta} = 60,
\end{equation}
where $x_{i,d}$ is the daily excess return and $\bar{x}_{i,t}$ its exponentially weighted mean. The centre of mass is 60 trading days, at least 120 observations are required, and the estimate dated $t$ uses data up to and including the close of $t$. Portfolio risk uses these volatilities with the trailing 252-day correlation matrix of daily excess returns, $\Sigma_t = D_t C_t D_t$.

\subsection{Strategy variants}
\label{sec:vt}

\textbf{V1: raw TSMOM.} Equal notional per instrument, $w_{i,t} = s_{i,t}/N$ with $N = 10$, so gross exposure is one.

\textbf{V2a: inverse-volatility weighting.} Each instrument receives a share $b_i$ of a risk budget, and its position is scaled so that its stand-alone ex-ante volatility equals that share of the 10\% target $\sigma^* $:
\begin{equation}
w^{\mathrm{2a}}_{i,t} = s_{i,t}\, b_i\, \frac{\sigma^*}{\sigma_{i,t}}, \qquad b_i = \frac{1}{4\,n_{c(i)}},
\end{equation}
where $n_{c(i)}$ is the number of instruments in the asset class of $i$. The risk budget is thus split equally across the four asset classes and then equally within each class. Because instruments within a class are highly correlated (Section~\ref{sec:data}), equal risk per instrument (the MOP convention) would give the near-duplicate equity and Treasury funds a multiple of their diversification value; it is reported as a robustness check. This allocation was revised from equal risk per instrument before any result was computed.

\textbf{V2: volatility targeting (primary).} V2a weights are rescaled so that the ex-ante volatility of the whole portfolio equals the target:
\begin{equation}
w^{\mathrm{2}}_{t} = \lambda_t\, w^{\mathrm{2a}}_{t}, \qquad \lambda_t = \frac{\sigma^*}{\sqrt{ w^{\mathrm{2a}\prime}_{t}\, \Sigma_t\, w^{\mathrm{2a}}_{t}}}.
\end{equation}
Leverage is not capped in the primary specification, mirroring the futures literature; caps of 2 and 3 times net asset value are robustness checks. The budgets fix stand-alone volatility, not risk contribution, so V2 is an inverse-volatility portfolio rather than an equal-risk-contribution portfolio, and correlated same-sign positions take more than their nominal share of risk (Table~\ref{tab:riskshares}).

\textbf{Benchmarks.} Long-only risk parity (LO-RP) applies the V2 construction with every signal set to $+1$, so it isolates what TSMOM adds beyond holding the same risk-balanced, leveraged book long; \citet{huang2020} argue that much of the TSMOM premium is of this static kind. SPY and a monthly-rebalanced 60/40 portfolio of SPY and IEF are passive benchmarks, reported without costs.

\subsection{Regime-conditioned exposure}
\label{sec:regime_method}

\textbf{Observation series.} The primary regime model (R1) is fitted to the weekly cross-asset trend payoff, which measures directly whether trend following is currently being rewarded. On each day $d$, every instrument with a twelve-month signal contributes its unit-risk TSMOM return $s_{i,m(d)}\, x_{i,d} / \sigma_{i,m(d)}$, where $m(d)$ is the latest month-end strictly before $d$ (the position actually held on day $d$). The daily payoff is the average over the available instruments, and the weekly payoff $y_w$ is its sum over the week. The alternative model (R2) is fitted to the weekly excess return of SPY, the textbook market-regime indicator, and tests the crisis-alpha thesis directly.

\textbf{Model.} Each series follows a two-state Gaussian Markov-switching model \citep{hamilton1989} with state-dependent mean and variance,
\begin{equation}
y_w = \mu_{S_w} + \sigma_{S_w}\, \varepsilon_w, \qquad \varepsilon_w \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1), \qquad \Pr(S_w = j \mid S_{w-1} = i) = p_{ij},
\end{equation}
estimated by maximum likelihood with 20 random starting values, the best of which is kept.

\textbf{Walk-forward estimation.} At every month-end $t$ the model is re-estimated on all weeks ending on or before $t$, and the regime signal is the \emph{filtered} probability $\Pr(S_w = j \mid y_1, \ldots, y_w)$ of the last such week. Filtered probabilities use only past and current observations. Smoothed probabilities, $\Pr(S_w = j \mid y_1, \ldots, y_T)$, condition on the whole sample and are never used for any reported result. Because state labels are arbitrary in estimation, they are re-identified at every refit: for R1 the target (``trending'') state is the one with the higher mean payoff; for R2 the target (``turbulent'') state is the one with the higher variance. At least 156 weeks are required. To have the overlay live for the 2008 crisis, the R1 model is trained from 2003 on whichever instruments already have signals, so the number of instruments in the payoff rises from two (SPY and EFA) in 2003 to all ten by April 2008. A version trained on main-sample weeks only, live from March 2011, is a robustness check.

\textbf{Overlay.} Variant 3 scales the V2 weights by an exposure multiplier,
\begin{equation}
w^{\mathrm{3}}_{t} = (0.5 + p_t)\, w^{\mathrm{2}}_{t},
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ is the target-state probability at $t$, so exposure varies between half and one and a half times V2. The portfolio is not re-targeted after the multiplier, which would cancel it.

\textbf{Lookahead illustration.} To quantify the bias that a common implementation error would create, the overlay is also computed from a single full-sample fit, using either its smoothed probabilities or its filtered probabilities. These variants use future data by construction; they are reported, clearly labelled, only as an illustration.

\subsection{Backtest engine and costs}

The backtest is a daily, self-financing simulation. With weights $w_{i,d-1}$ at the previous close, the portfolio's excess return on day $d$ is
\begin{equation}
R_d = \sum_i w_{i,d-1}\bigl(r_{i,d} - r^f_d\bigr) - c \sum_i \bigl| w^{\text{target}}_{i,d} - w_{i,d} \bigr| \mathbb{1}_{\text{trade}} - \text{fees}_d,
\end{equation}
and between trades the weights drift as $w_{i,d} = w_{i,d-1}(1 + r_{i,d})/(1 + r^f_d + R_d)$. Trading costs are $c = 10$ basis points per unit of notional traded, one way, with sensitivity from 0 to 50 basis points. This is conservative for these funds, whose quoted half-spreads are typically 1 to 5 basis points, but there is no market-impact model. Borrow fees on short positions and a financing spread over the bill rate on borrowed cash (long notional in excess of net asset value) are sensitivity analyses. Monthly returns compound the daily excess returns: $\prod_d (1 + r^f_d + R_d) - \prod_d (1 + r^f_d)$.

\subsection{Statistical inference}

Means are tested with Newey-West $t$-statistics using the automatic lag rule of \citet{newey1994}. Sharpe ratio standard errors follow \citet{lo2002}. Strategy variants built from the same signals are highly correlated, so comparing their Sharpe ratios as if they were independent is invalid; differences are instead tested by resampling each pair of monthly return series jointly with the stationary bootstrap of \citet{politis1994} (mean block length six months, 5,000 replications), in the spirit of \citet{ledoit2008}, with a two-sided $p$-value from the re-centred bootstrap distribution. Spanning regressions of a strategy on benchmark returns measure whether it earns a return beyond a static combination of them. The accuracy of the volatility forecast is measured by the bias statistic, the standard deviation of monthly returns divided by their ex-ante monthly volatility, which equals one for a correct forecast. Finally, the deflated Sharpe ratio of \citet{bailey2014} adjusts the primary Sharpe ratio for the number of configurations examined and for non-normal returns.

\subsection{Validation of the implementation}

The code base has 42 automated tests. Those most relevant to bias are truncation tests: signals, volatilities, portfolio weights, backtest returns and walk-forward regime probabilities are recomputed after deleting all data after a cut-off date (including dates in the middle of the March 2020 crash), and every value dated before the cut-off is required to be unchanged. The engine is checked against hand-calculated cases, the targeted portfolio's ex-ante volatility against an independent covariance calculation, and the gross V1 returns against the textbook formula $N^{-1}\sum_i s_{i,t-1} r_{i,t}$, which they match exactly.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Predictability at the instrument level}

Table~\ref{tab:predictive} reports pooled regressions of each instrument's volatility-scaled monthly excess return, $x_{i,t}/\sigma_{i,t-1}$, on its lagged signal, with standard errors clustered by month. The coefficients rise with the horizon, from $-0.001$ for the one-month signal to 0.016 for the twelve-month signal, the ordering found by MOP, but none is significant at 5\% (the twelve-month $t$-statistic is 1.66). With instrument fixed effects every $t$-statistic falls, to 1.02 for the twelve-month signal, because part of the pooled relation reflects differences in average returns across instruments rather than timing, the point made by \citet{huang2020}. Figure~\ref{fig:predictive} repeats MOP's lag-by-lag regressions: 2 of the 96 regressions (both at lag 8) exceed $|t| = 1.96$, fewer than the five that chance alone would produce. A decomposition of the twelve-month sign strategy's average return into a timing component and a static component (the product of average position and average return) attributes 56\% to timing. SPY is the exception, where the static long position in a rising market accounts for most of the profit.

\subsection{The three layers of performance}

Table~\ref{tab:perf} reports performance net of costs, and Figure~\ref{fig:cumulative} the cumulative returns. The raw strategy V1 earns 1.79\% a year with 7.01\% volatility, a Sharpe ratio of 0.26 (Newey-West $t = 1.05$). Inverse-volatility weighting (V2a) raises the Sharpe ratio to 0.36 at much lower volatility, and targeting 10\% portfolio volatility (V2) raises it to 0.45, with an annual excess return of 5.07\% and a Newey-West $t$-statistic of 1.95. Volatility targeting also changes the shape of the return distribution: V1's monthly skewness is $-0.50$, V2's is $+0.33$, and at a quarterly horizon V2's skewness is $+0.82$. Positive skewness is a signature of trend following, and it appears here only after volatility scaling. V1's negative skewness comes largely from 2009, when its equal-notional book was short equities into the March 2009 rebound, a classic momentum crash \citep{daniel2016}. Neither V1 nor V2 has positive skewness in daily returns, so the asymmetry builds over weeks rather than appearing in single days.

\subsection{Volatility targeting in practice}

V2 targets 10\% ex-ante volatility but realised 11.27\% (Table~\ref{tab:volval}), a bias statistic of 1.13. The forecasting error is not a code error: the ex-ante volatility of every rebalanced portfolio was verified independently. The overshoot comes from volatility jumps that a backward-looking estimator cannot anticipate, concentrated in September and October 2008, October 2014, February 2018 and March 2020. The daily data also contribute in the opposite direction: daily-annualised volatility overstates monthly-horizon equity risk (Table~\ref{tab:data}), which on its own would make the equity sleeve run below its budget. Figure~\ref{fig:vollev} shows twelve-month rolling realised volatility ranging from about 5\% to 17\%. The same overshoot appears in long-only risk parity (bias statistic 1.10), so it is a property of the risk model rather than of the trend signal.

\subsection{Is it trend, static exposure, or the futures factor?}

Table~\ref{tab:spanning} reports spanning regressions. Regressed on SPY, V2 has a slightly negative beta ($-0.10$) and an annualised alpha of 6.21\% ($t = 2.25$). This alpha $t$-statistic exceeds the $t$-statistic of V2's raw mean because the negative equity beta is credited for SPY's strong return; it should not be read as significant stand-alone alpha. Against long-only risk parity, the more demanding benchmark, the alpha falls to 4.78\% ($t = 1.76$). V2's correlation with LO-RP is only 0.09, so the trend signal is not a disguised static risk-parity position, but the comparison is noisy and the Sharpe difference between them is insignificant (Table~\ref{tab:sharpe}).

The 2020 result is instructive because it contradicts a common expectation, which was also my prior: that trend followers lose in fast crashes because their twelve-month signals are still long equities. An attribution of V2's return from 20 February to 23 March 2020 shows why it did not. The equity sleeve was mixed (long SPY, short EEM, with EFA switching from long to short at the end of February) and lost only 1.2\% in total, while the rest of the book was already positioned defensively: long Treasuries ($+6.6\%$), short the commodity basket ($+4.5\%$ from commodities) and long the dollar against the Australian dollar ($+2.5\%$ from currencies). Equal risk per asset class limited the damage from the one sleeve that was wrong. The same episode exposes the weakness of static risk parity: on 18 March 2020 equities, Treasuries, gold and commodities fell together in the scramble for cash, and LO-RP lost 6.8\% in a day.

Trend following tends to give back part of its crisis gains in the subsequent recovery, when its short equity positions are slow to reverse. V2 lost 5.7\% over the 2020 recovery leg (March to August 2020) and 8.7\% between the October 2022 trough and SPY's new high in December 2023.

\subsection{Trend following as a portfolio diversifier}
\label{sec:blend}

V2's Sharpe ratio of 0.45 is below those of SPY (0.72) and of the 60/40 portfolio (0.74), so the case for holding it cannot rest on its stand-alone performance. Trend following is usually held instead as a diversifier, and the crisis evidence above supports that role only indirectly. This subsection tests it directly. The analysis was added after the results above had been seen, and its specification was fixed and committed before it was computed. It uses V2 exactly as reported, so it is not a further trend configuration and does not enter the trial count of Section~\ref{sec:dsr}.

\textbf{Design.} The primary portfolio holds 80\% in the 60/40 benchmark and 20\% in V2; a secondary portfolio holds 80\% in SPY and 20\% in V2, the case of an equity-only investor. Allocations of 10\% to 20\% to managed futures are common in diversified portfolios, and 20\% is the round number at the top of that range; it was not chosen by comparing alternatives, and no other weight was computed. The two sleeves are reset to their target weights at each month-end rebalance date of the backtest engine and drift with their returns within the month, so that daily drawdowns are measured on the same basis as Table~\ref{tab:perf}; over each month the blend's wealth is then exactly the weighted sum of the two sleeves'. V2 is net of its 10 basis point trading costs while the benchmarks, as elsewhere in the paper, are gross. Rebalancing between the sleeves moves 0.13 of net asset value a year in the primary blend and 0.17 in the secondary (Table~\ref{tab:blend}), negligible beside V2's own turnover of 9.8, and no cost is deducted for it. Sharpe ratio differences against each blend's own benchmark use the paired stationary bootstrap of Table~\ref{tab:sharpe}. The specification names the effect on drawdowns and crisis losses as the headline, because that is the purpose of a diversifier.

\textbf{Results.} V2's monthly correlation with the 60/40 portfolio is $-0.11$, and with SPY $-0.14$. Adding it at 20\% lowers the 60/40 portfolio's volatility from 9.65\% to 7.81\% while lowering its mean excess return less, from 7.15\% to 6.74\%, so the Sharpe ratio rises from 0.74 to 0.86 and the Calmar ratio from 0.22 to 0.27. The maximum drawdown falls from 32.3\% to 25.3\%; both occur over the same months of 2008 to 2009, and the blend recovers about two weeks earlier. For the equity-only investor the maximum drawdown falls from 51.8\% to 42.7\%, and the Sharpe ratio rises from 0.72 to 0.80.

Table~\ref{tab:blendcrisis} shows the effect where it matters most. In each of the six crisis windows, for both investors, the blend lost less than its benchmark. The largest reductions came in the three episodes in which V2 itself made money: the 60/40 investor's loss fell from 32.3\% to 25.3\% in 2008 to 2009, from 18.9\% to 12.9\% in 2020 and from 21.6\% to 12.6\% in 2022. The reductions in 2011, 2018 and 2025, when V2 lost money, were small (0.8 to 1.4 percentage points) and come mainly from holding 20\% less of the benchmark. In SPY's worst decile of months the 60/40 investor's average loss fell from 4.7\% to 3.4\%. Figure~\ref{fig:blend} shows the full paths: the blend's drawdowns are shallower in 2008, 2020 and especially 2022, while its cumulative excess return finishes slightly below that of the 60/40 portfolio, the cost of its lower mean.

\textbf{Statistical significance.} The improvement in the Sharpe ratio is not significant for the primary blend: the difference is 0.12 with a bootstrap $p$-value of 0.08. For the equity-only blend the difference of 0.09 has $p = 0.04$, but two blends were tested, and a Bonferroni adjustment for the pair would put it at 0.08. The blend and its benchmark are highly correlated, which makes the test sharper than a comparison of independent Sharpe ratios, but 222 months still leave little power to establish a difference of this size. The evidence therefore supports a narrower claim than ``trend following improves a portfolio'': over this sample, a fixed 20\% allocation to V2 reduced drawdowns and crisis losses in every episode, at the cost of a lower mean return, and the accompanying rise in the risk-adjusted return is consistent with the diversification benefit but not statistically established.

\subsection{The regime overlay}
\label{sec:regime_results}

Table~\ref{tab:regime} evaluates the overlay. The primary version (V3, from the trend-payoff model R1) lowers the Sharpe ratio from 0.45 to 0.39 ($p = 0.32$). Regressed on V2, it has a beta of 1.12 and a negative, insignificant alpha: the overlay mainly raises average exposure (its mean multiplier is 1.19) without improving its timing. The SPY-based model R2 lowers the Sharpe ratio to 0.32. The model trained only on main-sample data does slightly better than the primary but still falls short of V2 (0.43, $p = 0.64$).

Two diagnostics explain the failure. First, the states are not what the labels suggest. In every non-degenerate refit (defined below), the higher-mean ``trending'' state of the trend payoff is also its lower-variance state: the model separates calm periods with a positive payoff from volatile periods with a slightly negative one, so R1 behaves largely as a volatility filter on the trend payoff. Second, the label does not forecast returns. Table~\ref{tab:regime_pred} regresses V2's next-month return on the probability: for the primary model the slope is negative ($t = -0.74$), and in months the model calls ``trending'' V2's Sharpe ratio is 0.33, against 0.80 in the others. For the main-sample model the slope is zero. Under the SPY model, V2 earns the same Sharpe ratio (0.45) in turbulent and calm months, so in this sample there is no evidence that trend returns are concentrated in high-volatility equity regimes. \textbf{Degenerate fits.} In 25 of the first 27 monthly refits of the primary model (March 2008 to June 2010), the maximum-likelihood estimate devotes one state to a handful of isolated extreme weeks: that state has a mean payoff of about $-0.27$ per week, against a full-sample weekly standard deviation of 0.07, and a probability of persisting of zero. This is a known weakness of maximum likelihood for mixtures of normal distributions, whose likelihood rewards fitting individual extreme observations \citep{kiefer1978,hamilton1991}. It arose here because the burn-in period contained a few extreme weeks (2 March 2007, 21 March 2003 and 19 May 2006, each a loss of between 0.25 and 0.33), the first of them when the payoff averaged only two instruments and the others when it averaged six. With the outlier absorbed, the remaining state was labelled ``trending'' with probability close to one, so V3 held 1.5 times V2's exposure through the 2008 to 2010 crisis. After June 2010 the longer history dominates and no further degenerate fits occur; the SPY model and the main-sample model have none at all.

The pre-registered primary result is reported unchanged. To show that the conclusion does not depend on these fits, a diagnostic variant, defined after the problem was found and labelled as such, flags a refit as degenerate when either state's probability of persisting is below 0.5 (an expected duration under two weeks) and sets the multiplier to one at flagged refits. Its Sharpe ratio is 0.42 ($p = 0.62$ against V2), and its predictive slope is still negative. Excluding the degenerate fits narrows the gap to V2 but does not reverse it, because the clean fits do not forecast trend returns either.

\textbf{The cost of lookahead.} The same overlay computed from full-sample smoothed probabilities would have reported a Sharpe ratio of 0.57, a gain of 0.12 over V2, a maximum drawdown of 18.7\% instead of 26.8\%, and a positive predictive slope. Using full-sample parameters with filtered probabilities produces no such gain (0.43). Almost all of the spurious improvement therefore comes from smoothing, which conditions each week's state on future observations, rather than from estimating the parameters on the full sample. An overlay that looks valuable in-sample and worthless out-of-sample is the typical signature of this error, and the gap of 0.18 between the smoothed and walk-forward versions measures how large it can be.

\subsection{Multiple testing}
\label{sec:dsr}

Twenty-five distinct TSMOM configurations were examined in this study: V1 at four horizons, V2a, four versions of the regime overlay and the sixteen rows of the robustness grid (the lookahead illustrations are excluded). The cross-sectional standard deviation of their Sharpe ratios is 0.17. Against this background, the probability that V2's true Sharpe ratio exceeds zero, adjusted for non-normality but not for selection, is 0.975. The deflated Sharpe ratio, which asks instead whether V2 beats the best Sharpe ratio expected from $N$ independent trials with no true skill, is 0.68 for $N = 25$ (Table~\ref{tab:dsr}). Because the configurations are highly correlated, the effective number of independent trials is well below 25; but even with $N = 2$ the deflated Sharpe ratio is 0.94, below the conventional 0.95 threshold. The primary result is therefore suggestive rather than statistically established, a conclusion consistent with the cautions of \citet{harvey2016} on backtested performance. The portfolio blends of Section~\ref{sec:blend} do not change this count: they hold V2 unchanged at a weight fixed in advance, so they test how the existing strategy behaves inside a portfolio rather than adding a new candidate trend strategy from which the best could be selected.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{ETFs are not futures.} The strategy is a long-short, leveraged portfolio of fully funded ETFs. A futures implementation would hold margin rather than full notional, finance leverage implicitly at close to the bill rate and short without a borrow fee. Here, V2's average gross leverage of 2.15 and short exposure of about one times net asset value would require a margin account, a borrow facility and financing above the bill rate. The fee sensitivities (Table~\ref{tab:fees}) show these costs can remove a quarter of the Sharpe ratio. Margin calls, recall risk on short positions and regulatory leverage limits on retail accounts are not modelled.

\textbf{Breadth.} Ten instruments, with an effective number of independent bets well below ten, are far fewer than the 58 in MOP or the hundreds traded by large managed-futures funds. Equities and Treasuries are each close to a single bet, and the three currency funds are correlated (Table~\ref{tab:corr}). Low breadth makes returns more concentrated and the statistics noisier; the shortfall against the AQR factor's Sharpe ratio is at least partly a breadth effect.

\textbf{One sample, one bond cycle.} The 18.5-year sample contains one long decline in interest rates followed by one sharp rise. Long Treasury positions contributed in the first part and short positions in 2022, and both helped. A sample with a different rate path could produce materially different results. More generally, 222 months give a Sharpe ratio standard error of about 0.23, so even a true Sharpe ratio of 0.5 would be hard to distinguish from zero; none of the comparisons between variants is significant, and the deflated Sharpe ratio is below the conventional threshold.

\textbf{Commodity fund mechanics.} The project specification noted that commodity ETFs carry roll-yield and contango effects that are not modelled. More precisely, DBC holds futures, so roll yield and contango are already in its returns, exactly as for a futures position, and because it holds bill collateral, subtracting the bill rate turns its total return into a close analogue of a futures excess return. The real differences are that DBC rolls according to an ``optimum yield'' rule rather than front-month rolling, and charges about 0.85\% a year. GLD holds physical gold, so it has no roll but carries storage cost through its expense ratio. Fund expenses are inside the returns of all ten ETFs, which is appropriate for an investable strategy but makes the comparison with futures slightly unfavourable.

\textbf{Costs and execution.} Costs are a flat 10 basis points per unit traded with no market impact, no dependence on volatility and no intraday execution. Trades are assumed to execute at the signal close. A one-day lag changes little, but trading at the close is itself an idealisation. At the scale of a personal account these assumptions are conservative for the equity and Treasury funds and less so for the smaller currency funds, whose trading volume is modest. No capacity analysis is attempted.

\textbf{Volatility forecast.} Realised volatility exceeded the target by 13\%, and the overshoot is concentrated in volatility jumps. A portfolio sized to a risk limit would have breached it in the episodes that matter most. The risk model uses a 60-day exponentially weighted volatility and a trailing one-year correlation matrix; it cannot anticipate jumps or a sudden change in correlations such as March 2020, when bonds and equities fell together.

\textbf{Regime model.} The Markov-switching model has limited power with weekly data over about 20 years, its states separate mainly on variance rather than mean, and its maximum-likelihood estimates were degenerate in 25 early refits. Burn-in composition also changes: the trend payoff averages over two to ten instruments before April 2008, so its scale is not constant. Alternative specifications (fat-tailed errors, a minimum regime duration, normalising the payoff by the number of instruments, more states) were not explored, because choosing among them after seeing results would be a specification search. The negative result applies to this specification and should not be read as evidence against regime conditioning in general.

\textbf{Multiple testing and researcher choices.} The design was fixed in advance, and every configuration examined is reported. Some choices, nonetheless, were made with knowledge of the literature's results on longer samples, such as the twelve-month horizon and the 60-day volatility estimator, and three elements were added after results were seen: the degenerate-fit diagnostic, the continuous-signal specification and the portfolio blends of Section~\ref{sec:blend} (whose specification was committed before they were computed). All three are identified in the text, none changes a primary result, and the deflated Sharpe ratio accounts for the full set of configurations.

\textbf{Portfolio application.} The 20\% blend weight is fixed rather than optimised, and no other weight was tested, so the blend results say nothing about the best allocation; an optimised weight would be fitted to this sample. The benchmarks are gross of costs while V2 is net, which slightly favours the benchmarks, and the blend's own rebalancing is not charged. The diversification benefit rests on V2's negative correlation with equities over a sample with one long bond rally and one sharp rate rise, the same episodes that drive V2's crisis gains; with a different rate path, or with different crises, the drawdown reductions could be smaller. The Sharpe ratio gain is not significant for the primary blend.

\textbf{Data.} Prices are from a free source (Yahoo Finance). The total-return series was verified against distributions and every large move was checked, but the data are not from an institutional vendor. The universe was chosen in 2026 from funds that exist today. All ten were among the largest and most liquid in their categories throughout the sample, so survivorship bias is limited, but it is not zero: a fund that might have been chosen in 2008 and later closed would not be in the universe.

\section{Conclusion}

A time-series momentum strategy built from ten liquid ETFs earned a positive return over 2008 to 2026, and volatility targeting was the layer that made it useful: it raised the Sharpe ratio from 0.26 to 0.45, turned negative skewness positive and balanced risk across asset classes. The volatility-targeted strategy delivered the property that justifies trend following in a portfolio, gaining in each of the three largest equity drawdowns of the sample, and it tracks the AQR futures factor closely enough to confirm that the ETF implementation captures the same phenomenon. The improvement from volatility targeting is, however, not statistically significant, and after accounting for the configurations examined, neither is the strategy's Sharpe ratio. Held at a pre-specified 20\% beside a 60/40 portfolio, the strategy reduced the maximum drawdown from 32.3\% to 25.3\% and cut the loss in every equity crisis window of the sample, while the rise in the portfolio's Sharpe ratio, from 0.74 to 0.86, is not statistically significant.

The regime overlay did not help. Its walk-forward versions lowered the Sharpe ratio, its ``trending'' state was mainly a low-volatility state with no forecasting power, and its early estimates were degenerate. The same overlay with smoothed probabilities would have appeared to add 0.12 to the Sharpe ratio, which illustrates how much of the apparent value of regime models in finance can be an artefact of using future information.

The honest summary is that the evidence supports trend following as a crisis diversifier with modest and statistically fragile stand-alone returns over this period. Natural extensions are a broader futures universe to raise breadth, a comparison of volatility estimators that react to jumps, and regime models with fat-tailed errors evaluated on a longer history.

\section*{Reproducibility}

{\raggedright
All code is in the project repository. After the data downloads (\texttt{fetch\_prices.py}, \texttt{fetch\_rates.py}, \texttt{fetch\_benchmarks.py}), \texttt{run\_all.py} rebuilds every result in order: \texttt{check\_data.py}, \texttt{run\_stage2.py} (signals, volatility and predictive regressions), \texttt{run\_stage3.py} (engine and V1), \texttt{run\_stage4.py} (volatility targeting and benchmarks), \texttt{run\_stage5.py} (regime overlay), \texttt{run\_stage6.py} (evaluation, crisis, costs, robustness and deflated Sharpe ratio), \texttt{run\_blend.py} (portfolio blends), \texttt{make\_tables.py} and \texttt{make\_figures.py}. Every parameter, including the blend specification, is in \texttt{config.py}. Every table and figure is generated from the saved outputs, so no reported number is transcribed by hand.\par}
